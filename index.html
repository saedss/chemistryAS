Part 0 · start here, before anything else

What do 1s, 2s and 2p mean?

You will see things like 1s² 2s² 2p² all through this chapter. It looks like code, but it is just an address. It tells you where an atom's electrons live. Once you can read it, hybridization becomes much easier.

0.1An atom is like a small hotel

Picture this

The nucleus is the reception desk in the middle. The electrons are the guests. The hotel has floors (floor 1, floor 2, floor 3...). Each floor has rooms. Every room has only 2 beds, so a room holds at most 2 electrons. Guests always take the lowest free floor first, because it is closest to reception and needs the least energy.

Hotel wordChemistry wordWhat it means
FloorShell (1, 2, 3...)How far from the nucleus. Higher number = further away = more energy.
RoomOrbitalA space for electrons. Holds at most 2.
Type of rooms or pAn s room is on its own (1 room). p rooms come in a set of 3.

0.2Reading one piece: 2p²

2p2
2

The big number is the floor (shell). Here: floor 2.

p

The letter is the type of room. Here: the p rooms (there are 3 of them).

²

The small raised number is how many electrons are there. Here: 2 electrons.

So 2p² just means: "on floor 2, in the p rooms, there are 2 electrons." And 1s² 2s² 2p² is a list of all the electrons: 2 on floor 1 in the s room, 2 on floor 2 in the s room, and 2 on floor 2 in the p rooms. Add the small numbers: 2 + 2 + 2 = 6 electrons. That is carbon.

Floor 1

Only an s room: 1s.

Holds 2 electrons.

Floor 2

One s room (2s) and three p rooms (2p).

Holds 2 + 6 = 8 electrons. That is where the "octet" (8) comes from.

Floor 3

One s room (3s) and three p rooms (3p), and more (d rooms) you do not need yet.

0.3Three filling rules

  1. How many electrons? The same as the atomic number on the periodic table. Carbon is element 6, so it has 6 electrons.
  2. Fill from the bottom. The order is 1s, then 2s, then 2p, then 3s, then 3p. Each s takes up to 2. Each set of p takes up to 6.
  3. In the p rooms, spread out first. Put one electron in each p room before any room gets a second one. Like people on a bus who pick an empty double seat before sitting next to a stranger. Two electrons in one room spin opposite ways, drawn ↑↓.

0.5Why this matters for bonding

Only the top floor bonds

The outer shell (the highest floor with electrons) is the only one used in bonding. For carbon that is floor 2: 2s² 2p². The 1s² pair stays out of it. That is why the diagrams in Part 5 ignore 1s.

Single electrons make bonds

An electron alone in a room (one arrow, not two) can pair up with an electron from another atom. That pair is a bond. Carbon has 2 rooms with a single electron, which is the puzzle Part 5 solves.

Part 1 · notes page 60

What is bonding?

Bonding is what holds particles together. Every bond in this chapter is the same basic idea: something negative is pulled toward something positive. The three types differ only in what is pulled to what.

Ionic

Electrons are passed over

A metal atom gives electrons to a non-metal atom. That makes a positive and a negative ion.

pull: + ions ↔ − ions

Covalent

Electrons are shared

Two non-metal atoms each put one electron into a shared pair.

pull: shared pair ↔ both nuclei

Metallic

Electrons are set free

Metal atoms let go of their outer electrons into a shared "sea" that flows around the ions.

pull: + metal ions ↔ sea of electrons

Quick way to tell:metal + non-metal → ionicnon-metal + non-metal → covalentmetal on its own → metallic

Part 2 · notes pages 60 to 64

Ionic bonding

Ionic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions in a lattice. Below: how ions form, what charge they get, how to write formulas, and why ionic compounds behave the way they do.

2.1How ions form

Atoms are most stable with a full outer shell, like the noble gases (He, Ne, Ar). There are two ways to get one:

  • Metals lose electrons. They have only 1, 2 or 3 outer electrons, so giving them away is easier. Losing negative electrons leaves a positive ion.
  • Non-metals gain electrons. They are only a few electrons short of a full shell, so taking a few more is easier. Gaining negative electrons makes a negative ion.

Hydrogen is unusual. It normally forms H⁺, but it can also form the hydride ion H⁻.

Metal

Mg 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s²

loses 2 electrons →

Mg²⁺ 1s² 2s² 2p⁶

12 protons, 10 electrons. Charge = 12 − 10 = 2+

Non-metal

O 1s² 2s² 2p⁴

gains 2 electrons →

O²⁻ 1s² 2s² 2p⁶

8 protons, 10 electrons. Charge = 8 − 10 = 2−

Both ions end up with the same electron arrangement as neon.

2.4The giant ionic lattice

In a solid, ions do not pair up one to one. Millions of them stack in a regular 3D pattern called a .

In sodium chloride, each Na⁺ (orange, small) is surrounded by 6 Cl⁻ (blue, large), and each Cl⁻ by 6 Na⁺. The glowing ions in the middle show this.

The ionic bond is the pull between each ion and all the opposite ions around it, in every direction.

Careful in exams. The sticks only help you see the pattern. They are not bonds. Press Space filling to see how the ions really pack together.

Not every ionic compound has this same pattern (page 63).

2.5Properties of ionic compounds

High melting point

There are many strong attractions between ions. It takes a lot of energy to break them all.

Conducts only when melted or dissolved

Electricity needs charged particles that can move. In the solid, the ions are locked in place. Melted or dissolved, they are free to move.

Brittle

A knock shifts one layer. Now + sits next to + and − next to −. They repel and the crystal splits. Watch below.

Usually dissolves in water

Water can pull the ions apart. Ionic compounds usually do not dissolve in organic solvents.

2.6Ion size and bond strength

Positive ions are smaller than their atoms. They lose their whole outer shell. The same number of protons now pulls on fewer electrons, so they are held closer.

Negative ions are bigger than their atoms. They have extra electrons but the same protons. The pull is shared between more electrons, so each one is held less tightly.

Same electrons, more protons → smaller. The six ions in the picture all have 10 electrons (like neon). Going from N³⁻ to Al³⁺ the number of protons rises, so the ions shrink.

Down a group, ions get bigger, because each one has an extra shell of electrons.

Stronger ionic bonding = smaller ions and bigger charges. They get closer and pull harder. MgO (Mg²⁺ and O²⁻) melts at about 2850 °C. NaCl (Na⁺ and Cl⁻) melts at 801 °C.

Covalent bonding

When two non-metals meet, both want electrons, so neither gives any away. They share instead.

3.1Sharing a pair

Each atom puts in one electron. The two electrons make a shared pair, and that shared pair is the .

Both nuclei (positive) pull on the shared pair (negative). That pull holds the two atoms together. A covalent bond is strong.

We only draw the outer electrons, in pairs. A is shared between two atoms. A belongs to one atom and is not in a bond. Lone pairs matter a lot for shapes (Part 6).

Ammonia, NH₃, three ways. In the dot and cross diagram, crosses are nitrogen's electrons and dots are hydrogen's. N has 3 bonding pairs and 1 lone pair.

3.2The octet rule and multiple bonds

After bonding, most atoms have 8 electrons in their outer shell, like a noble gas. This is the octet rule. Hydrogen is the exception: it only needs 2. Sometimes atoms share more than one pair:

Cl–Cl

Single bond: 1 shared pair

O=O

Double bond: 2 shared pairs

N≡N

Triple bond: 3 shared pairs

O=C=O

CO₂: two double bonds. C ends with 8, each O with 8.

3.3When the octet rule breaks

Fewer than 8

Be, B and Al can end up short. In BCl₃ boron has only 6 outer electrons. In BeCl₂ beryllium has only 4. They often fix this later with a dative bond (3.5).

More than 8: expanding the octet

Atoms in period 3 and below (P, S, Cl and others) can hold more than 8. In PF₅ phosphorus has 10. In SF₆ sulfur has 12. They can do this because they have empty d orbitals.

Period 2 atoms (C, N, O) cannot: there are no 2d orbitals.

3.5Dative covalent bonds

In a normal covalent bond each atom gives one electron. In a (also called a co-ordinate bond) both electrons come from the same atom, from one of its lone pairs.

  • The giver needs a lone pair.
  • The receiver needs an empty space in its outer shell.
  • Draw it as an arrow, pointing away from the atom that gives the pair.

Once it has formed, a dative bond is exactly the same as any other covalent bond. In NH₄⁺ you cannot tell which N–H bond was dative.

H₃O⁺ and NH₄⁺

H₂O or NH₃ gives a lone pair to H⁺, which has no electrons at all.

NH₃ → BCl₃

N gives its lone pair to boron, which only had 6 outer electrons. Now boron has 8.

Al₂Cl₆

Two AlCl₃ molecules join. A Cl on each gives a lone pair to the other Al: two dative bonds.

CO

Carbon monoxide has a triple bond, C≡O. One of the three bonds is dative, from O to C.

BeCl₂ in the solid

Cl lone pairs give to Be atoms of the next molecule. This makes a long chain (a polymer).

NO₃⁻

The nitrate ion has a double bond, a single bond and a dative bond. They all end up equal in strength.

Bond strength: energy and length

Bond energy

The energy needed to break one mole of a bond in gas molecules into gas atoms. Bigger number = stronger bond.

Bond length

The distance between the two nuclei. Shorter bonds are stronger, because the shared pair is closer to both nuclei and is pulled harder.

4.2Double and triple bonds are shorter and stronger

A double or triple bond has more electrons between the two nuclei. More negative charge between them means a stronger pull, so the atoms sit closer together.

But notice: C=C is not twice as strong as C–C. The second bond (the π bond, Part 5) is weaker than the first.

Hybridization, step by step

★ Take this part slowly

Hybridization sounds scary, but it is only three small steps. Each step below has a picture, an everyday comparison and a quick check. Do not move on until the check makes sense.

1 Promote→2 Mix→3 Spread out

5.1Four ideas you need first

Hybridization is built on these. If 1s, 2s and 2p still look strange, go back to Part 0 first.

5.2The problem with carbon

Use idea ④ on carbon. Carbon is 1s² 2s² 2p². In its box diagram the 2s pair is full, and only the two 2p electrons are single. So carbon should make only 2 bonds, at 90° (p orbitals sit at right angles).

But real methane is CH₄. It has 4 bonds, all exactly the same, at 109.5°.

So the simple idea gives the wrong answer for carbon. Hybridization fixes it.

Is it real? Hybridization is a model: a way of describing orbitals that matches what we measure. Atoms do not "decide" anything. But the model predicts shapes and angles correctly, which is why you learn it.

5.3Step 1 · Promote

What happens. One of the two 2s electrons jumps up into the empty 2p box. This is called .

Like this

Two people share a room downstairs, and one room upstairs is empty. One person moves upstairs. Now everybody has a room to themselves, and each can pair up with a visitor.

Does it cost energy? A little, because 2p is slightly higher than 2s. But now carbon has 4 single electrons, so it can make 4 bonds instead of 2. Every bond releases energy, so the deal pays off.

Result: 1s² 2s¹ 2p³

5.4Step 2 · Mix

What happens. The 2s orbital and some of the 2p orbitals blend together. They make brand-new orbitals that are all exactly the same. These are . "Hybridize" just means "mix".

Like this

Mixing paint. One tin of yellow (the s orbital) plus three tins of blue (the p orbitals) makes four tins of the same green. You cannot tell which tin was yellow any more. All four greens are identical. Try it: choose how many p orbitals go in.

Reading the name. The name is the recipe. sp³ means s¹p³: one s and three p. The small number counts the p orbitals that went in. Add 1 for the s and you get how many hybrids come out.

Orbitals in = orbitals out. Mix 4, get 4. Mix 3, get 3. Mix 2, get 2.

Shape of a hybrid: one big lobe and one small lobe. The big lobe points to where the bond will be.

5.5Step 3 · Spread out

What happens. Each hybrid orbital holds negative electrons, so the hybrids push each other away. They spread out until they are as far apart as possible.

Like this

Tie some balloons together by their knots. They push apart and settle into exactly these shapes. Press a button to watch the lobes start bunched up and push apart.

  • 2 hybrids → straight line, 180° (linear)
  • 3 hybrids → flat triangle, 120° (trigonal planar)
  • 4 hybrids → tetrahedron, 109.5° (tetrahedral)

Then each big lobe overlaps head-on with an orbital from another atom. That makes a .

5.7Leftover p orbitals make π bonds

In sp² and sp atoms, some p orbitals were not mixed. They stick out at right angles. When two of them sit side by side, they overlap sideways and make a . This is how double and triple bonds form.

Ethene, C₂H₄ (page 71). Each carbon is sp². Press Replay: two CH₂ pieces move in, their sp² lobes meet head-on (the σ bond), then the two upright p orbitals overlap sideways (the π bond). The π bond is a cloud above and below the molecule.

Now twist it. As one end turns, the p orbitals stop being side by side, so the overlap shrinks. At 90° the π bond is broken. That costs about 260 kJ mol⁻¹, so at room temperature a C=C bond does not rotate. A single bond (σ only) rotates freely.

Ethyne, C₂H₂. Each carbon is sp, with two leftover p orbitals. They make two π bonds, so the carbons share a triple bond: 1σ + 2π.

σ bond

  • Head-on overlap
  • Electrons on the line between nuclei
  • Stronger
  • Allows rotation
  • Every single bond is one σ

π bond

  • Sideways overlap of two p orbitals
  • Electrons above and below the line
  • Weaker
  • Stops rotation
  • Double = 1σ + 1π, triple = 1σ + 2π

    Shapes of molecules

    Electron pairs are all negative, so they repel each other. Around the central atom they spread out as far apart as possible. So the shape depends on how many pairs there are, both bonding pairs and lone pairs.

    6.1The five basic shapes

    This is the same idea as step 3 of hybridization. For 2, 3 and 4 pairs you get exactly the same shapes. Shapes with 5 and 6 pairs need an expanded octet, so only atoms from period 3 down can make them.

    6.2Count the pairs (the table method)

    This is the table from page 75. It works for molecules with single bonds. Pick an example, or build your own.

    6.3Lone pairs push harder

    A lone pair is held by only one nucleus, so it is fatter and sits closer to the central atom. It pushes harder than a bonding pair.

    That squeezes the bonds closer together. Each lone pair makes the bond angle about 2.5° smaller.

    CH₄ 109.5° → NH₃ 107° → H₂O 104.5°. All three have 4 pairs, so they start from a tetrahedron.

    6.4How to explain a shape in an exam

    1. State the number of bonding pairs and lone pairs.
    2. Say that electron pairs repel and get as far apart as possible.
    3. If there are no lone pairs: the pairs repel equally.
    4. If there are lone pairs: lone pairs repel more than bonding pairs.
    5. State the shape and the bond angle.
    Model answer · NH₃

    N has 3 bonding pairs and 1 lone pair. Electron pairs repel and get as far apart as possible. The lone pair repels more than the bonding pairs, so it pushes the N–H bonds closer together. The shape is trigonal pyramidal with a bond angle of 107°.

    6.5Double and triple bonds count as one

    One region each

    For shape, a double or triple bond acts like a single bond. It is one region of negative charge. CO₂ (O=C=O) has 2 regions, so it is linear, like BeCl₂.

    Draw it first

    The table method struggles with double bonds. Draw the dot and cross diagram first. In a neutral molecule, oxygens are usually double bonded to the central atom. In a negative ion, the extra electrons sit on oxygens, which are then single bonded.

    Examples

    SO₄²⁻: 2 S=O and 2 S–O⁻, 4 regions, no lone pairs → tetrahedral.

    NO₃⁻, CO₃²⁻: 3 regions → trigonal planar.

    Part 7 · notes pages 84 to 85

    Electronegativity and polarity

    Atoms in a covalent bond share a pair of electrons, but not always equally. Think of a tug of war over the shared pair.

    7.1What electronegativity means

    Electronegativity is how strongly an atom in a covalent bond pulls the shared electrons toward itself.

    Fluorine is the strongest puller (4.0). F, O, N and Cl are the most electronegative atoms.

    Across a period it goes up. More protons pull on the same shell, and the atom gets smaller, so the shared electrons are pulled harder.

    Down a group it goes down. The outer electrons are further from the nucleus, and more inner shells shield them.

    Group 1increases across →Group 7

    Darker = pulls harder. Values go down each column. Tap two elements to set up the tug of war below.

    7.2Tug of war: polar bonds

    If both atoms pull equally (H–H, Cl–Cl), the bond is non-polar. If one pulls harder, the shared pair sits closer to it. That end gets a small negative charge, δ−, and the other end δ+. This is a polar covalent bond, or a . δ just means "a little bit". If the difference is very big, the electron is pulled right over and you get ions instead.

    7.3Polar and non-polar molecules

    A molecule can have polar bonds and still be non-polar overall. If the shape is symmetrical and all the bonds are the same, the pulls cancel, like a tug of war with equal teams on every side.

    If the molecule is not symmetrical (different bonds, or lone pairs on the central atom), the pulls do not cancel. The molecule is polar.

    Part 8 · notes pages 82 to 83

    Metallic bonding

    In a metal, each atom lets go of its outer electrons. The atoms become positive ions packed in a giant lattice. The freed electrons are : they belong to no single atom and move through the whole metal, like a sea.

    Metallic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between the positive metal ions and the delocalized electrons.

    8.1What makes it stronger?

    1. More protons in the nucleus pull harder.
    2. More delocalized electrons per atom: more negative charge in the sea.
    3. Smaller ions (with a bigger charge) let the electrons get closer.

    Sodium gives 1 electron per atom. Magnesium gives 2 and its ion is smaller. So magnesium has stronger metallic bonding and a much higher melting point. Aluminium gives 3, so its boiling point is higher still.

    Press Connect a battery: the delocalized electrons drift toward the positive end. That flow is an electric current.

    8.2Properties of metals

    High melting point

    The attraction between the ions and the electron sea is strong. It takes a lot of energy to break.

    Conduct electricity

    The delocalized electrons can move through the whole structure in any direction.

    Conduct heat

    Fast-moving electrons bump into slower ones and pass their energy along.

    Malleable and ductile

    Malleable: can be hammered into shape. Ductile: can be pulled into wires. All the ions are the same, so layers slide over each other and the sea still holds them. Watch below.

    Shiny

    The delocalized electrons reflect light.

    Compare with ionic

    Hit an ionic crystal and like charges meet, so it shatters (2.5). Hit a metal and it bends.
