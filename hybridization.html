Hybridization, step by step

This guide takes hybridization apart into small pieces and explains each one in plain words, with a picture you can play with. Twelve short lessons. Go in order, and do not skip the quick checks.

New to 1s, 2s and 2p? Read Part 0 on the main page first. It takes five minutes and makes everything here easier. Tap any for a simple meaning.

Lesson 1

The question this guide answers

The whole story in one minute

  1. The problem. Carbon has only 2 single electrons, so it "should" make 2 bonds. But in methane it makes 4 identical bonds at 109.5°.
  2. Step 1, promote. One electron moves up from 2s to the empty 2p. Now carbon has 4 single electrons, so it can make 4 bonds.
  3. Step 2, mix. The 2s and the three 2p orbitals blend into 4 brand-new, identical orbitals called sp³.
  4. Step 3, spread out. The 4 new orbitals push each other away and point to the corners of a tetrahedron, 109.5° apart.
  5. Step 4, bond. Each one overlaps with a hydrogen orbital. The two electrons pair up and make a bond.

Do not worry if this does not click yet. Each lesson below takes one of these lines and explains it slowly.

Carbon's address is 1s² 2s² 2p². In box form, the 2s room has a pair (↑↓), and two of the three 2p rooms have one electron each (↑ ↑). The third 2p room is empty.

A bond needs one single electron from each atom. Carbon has only two single electrons. So on paper it should make two bonds, at 90°, because p orbitals sit at right angles.

But scientists measured methane. It has four bonds. All four are the same length and the same strength. Every angle between them is 109.5°. The simple picture is wrong, and we need a better one.

Lesson 2

Meet carbon's outer orbitals in 3D

Before we change anything, look at what carbon starts with. Its outer shell (floor 2) has one 2s orbital and three 2p orbitals.

The 2s orbital (yellow) is a ball around the nucleus. It has no direction: it is the same in every direction.

The three 2p orbitals (blue) are dumbbells. Each one points along its own line: x (left–right), y (up–down) and z (front–back). They are all at 90° to each other, like the corner of a box.

Tap the buttons to show one orbital at a time. Spin the model to see that the three p orbitals really are at right angles.

Remember: an orbital is a space where electrons can be. It can hold 0, 1 or 2 electrons. The shape is the same whether it is empty or full.

What if carbon did not mix them?

Imagine carbon only did step 1 (promotion) and then bonded with its orbitals exactly as they are.

  • Three H atoms would bond to the three p orbitals. Those bonds would be at 90°.
  • The fourth H would bond to the round s orbital. That bond would be different: shorter and pointing somewhere else.

That is not what methane looks like. Press Promote and mix to see the real thing: four identical bonds at 109.5°.

The big idea: promotion fixes the number of bonds (4). Mixing fixes the shape and makes all four bonds identical. You need both.

Lesson 3

Step 1 · Promote

What happens. One of the two electrons in the 2s room moves up into the empty 2p room. This is called .

Before: 1s² 2s² 2p² with 2 single electrons.
After: 1s² 2s¹ 2p³ with 4 single electrons.

Like this

Two people share a room downstairs. One room upstairs is empty. One person moves upstairs. Now four people each have their own room, and each can pair up with a visitor (a hydrogen electron).

Why does it cost energy?

2p is a little higher in energy than 2s, like a higher floor. Moving an electron up takes energy.

So why bother?

With 4 single electrons, carbon can make 2 extra bonds. Every bond releases energy. Two extra C–H bonds give back much more than promotion cost (see the chart).

Why the 2s electron, not 1s?

1s is an inner shell, far lower in energy. Lifting an electron out of it would cost far too much. Only outer (floor 2) electrons take part.

Why into 2p, not 3s?

2p is the nearest empty room. Going up to floor 3 would cost much more energy.

Lesson 4

Step 2 · Mix

This is the step most students find strange. "Hybridize" just means "mix". The 2s and the 2p orbitals blend together to make new orbitals that are all exactly the same.

Why the new orbital is lopsided

Orbitals are really waves. Each lobe of a p orbital is one side of the wave. One lobe is "up" (+) and the other is "down" (−). The s orbital is "up" (+) everywhere.

When you mix them, they add together:

  • On the + side: up + up = bigger. That lobe grows.
  • On the − side: down + up = they partly cancel. That lobe shrinks.

So a hybrid orbital has one big lobe and one small lobe. The big lobe sticks out a long way in one direction, which makes it very good at reaching another atom and making a strong bond.

You do not need the + and − signs in an exam. They just explain the lopsided shape.

How many orbitals mix? The paint analogy

Like this

One tin of yellow paint (the s orbital) plus three tins of blue paint (the p orbitals) gives four tins of the same green. You cannot tell which tin was yellow any more. Choose how many p orbitals go in.

How do I read the name?

The name is the recipe. sp³ = s¹p³ = one s + three p. The small number counts the p orbitals that went in. Add 1 for the s to get the number of hybrids: sp³ → 4, sp² → 3, sp → 2.

Orbitals in = orbitals out

Mixing never creates or destroys orbitals. Put 4 in, get 4 out. Put 3 in, get 3 out, and the unused p orbital stays exactly as it was.

Where are they in energy?

The new hybrids sit between 2s and 2p, all at the same level. You can see this in the box diagram in Lesson 7: the hybrid boxes sit in a row between the 2s and 2p lines.

How does the atom "choose" how many?

It mixes one orbital for each σ bond and each lone pair it needs. Carbon in CH₄ needs 4 → sp³. Carbon in ethene needs 3 → sp², and keeps one p for the π bond (Lesson 8).

Lesson 5

Step 3 · Spread out

The new hybrid orbitals all contain negative electrons. Negative pushes negative away. So the hybrids spread out until they are as far apart as possible.

Like this

Tie balloons together by their knots. They push each other away and settle into exactly these shapes. Press a button: the lobes start bunched up, then push apart.

  • 2 hybrids → straight line, 180° (linear)
  • 3 hybrids → flat triangle, 120° (trigonal planar)
  • 4 hybrids → tetrahedron, 109.5° (tetrahedral)

Why 109.5° and not 90°?

On paper, four arms in a flat cross look evenly spread: 90° apart. But a molecule is 3D.

If the arms tilt out of the flat page, they can get further apart. The best they can do is point to the corners of a tetrahedron (a triangle-based pyramid). Then every pair of arms is 109.5° apart, which is more than 90°.

Further apart means less pushing, so the tetrahedron wins. Switch between the two buttons and spin the model to compare.

Lesson 6

Step 4 · Overlap and bond

Each of carbon's four sp³ orbitals has one electron. Each hydrogen has one electron in its 1s orbital (the yellow ball).

Watch the model. A hydrogen flies in along the line of a big green lobe. Its 1s orbital overlaps head-on with the tip of the lobe (the pink spot). The two electrons pair up in the overlap. That shared pair is the covalent bond.

A bond made by head-on overlap is called a . The shared electrons sit right on the line between the two nuclei.

Why all four bonds are identical: the four sp³ orbitals are identical, and each one meets an identical hydrogen. So the four bonds have the same length, the same strength and the same 109.5° angles. That matches the measurements.

Lesson 8 · notes pages 69 and 71

Leftover p orbitals make π bonds

In sp² and sp atoms, some p orbitals were not mixed. They stick out at right angles to the hybrids. When two of them sit side by side on neighboring atoms, they overlap sideways. That is a .

Ethene: one σ and one π

Each carbon in ethene is sp²: three hybrids in a flat triangle, plus one leftover p orbital standing straight up.

  1. The two carbons meet. One sp² lobe from each overlaps head-on: the C–C σ bond.
  2. Each carbon's other two sp² lobes bond to H atoms: four C–H σ bonds.
  3. The two upright p orbitals are now side by side. They overlap sideways, above and below the molecule: the π bond.

So the double bond C=C is 1 σ + 1 π. Press Replay to watch it form.

Twist it. As one end turns, the p orbitals stop being side by side, so the π overlap shrinks. At 90° it is gone: the π bond has broken. That is why a C=C bond cannot rotate at room temperature.

Ethyne: one σ and two π

Each carbon in ethyne, H–C≡C–H, is sp: two hybrids in a straight line, plus two leftover p orbitals, at right angles to each other.

The sp lobes make the σ bonds (C–H and C–C), all in one straight line, 180°.

The leftover p orbitals pair up sideways twice: one π bond above and below, and a second π bond in front and behind. So the triple bond is 1 σ + 2 π.

Pattern: single bond = 1 σ. Double bond = 1 σ + 1 π. Triple bond = 1 σ + 2 π. The first bond between two atoms is always σ.

Lesson 9

Lone pairs live in hybrid orbitals too

Nitrogen in NH₃ and oxygen in H₂O are also sp³. But they work a little differently from carbon, and this confuses a lot of students.

No promotion needed

N already has 3 single electrons (2p³). O already has 2. Promotion would not give them any extra single electrons, so they skip it.

Still mix all four

They still mix 2s + three 2p into four sp³ orbitals. That is what gives the tetrahedral starting shape.

Pairs stay paired

A hybrid with two electrons is a lone pair. A hybrid with one electron makes a bond. Lone pairs push harder, so the angles shrink: 107° in NH₃, 104.5° in H₂O.

Lesson 11

Writing it in an exam

Examiners give marks for each step, in order. Use the same pattern every time: ground state, promote, mix, shape, bonds.

Methane, CH₄ (sp³)

C ground state is 1s² 2s² 2p², with only 2 unpaired electrons. One 2s electron is promoted to the empty 2p orbital: 1s² 2s¹ 2p³, 4 unpaired electrons. The 2s and three 2p orbitals hybridize into four identical sp³ orbitals. These repel equally and point to the corners of a tetrahedron. Each overlaps head-on with an H 1s orbital to form a σ bond. Shape tetrahedral, 109.5°.

Boron trichloride, BCl₃ (sp²)

B ground state is 1s² 2s² 2p¹, with 1 unpaired electron. One 2s electron is promoted: 1s² 2s¹ 2p², 3 unpaired electrons. The 2s and two 2p orbitals hybridize into three identical sp² orbitals; one empty 2p orbital is left. The sp² orbitals repel equally into a flat triangle. Each forms a σ bond with Cl. Shape trigonal planar, 120°.

Ethene, C₂H₄ (sp² with a π bond)

Each C is promoted to 1s² 2s¹ 2p³. The 2s and two 2p orbitals hybridize into three sp² orbitals; one 2p is left unhybridized. The sp² orbitals lie in a plane at 120°. They form σ bonds with two H and with the other C by head-on overlap. The unhybridized 2p orbitals overlap sideways, above and below the plane, to form the π bond. C=C is one σ and one π.

Ammonia, NH₃ (sp³ with a lone pair)

N is 1s² 2s² 2p³, with 3 unpaired electrons. The 2s and three 2p orbitals hybridize into four sp³ orbitals. One holds a lone pair; three hold one electron each. Each of these overlaps with an H 1s orbital to form a σ bond. The lone pair repels more than bonding pairs, so the shape is trigonal pyramidal, 107°.
